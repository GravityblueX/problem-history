# M1 candidate packet — “文学有什么用？”

> Status: **surveyed as an M1 candidate, not selected as the final pilot**.
>
> Branch context: companion evidence packet for `research/pilot-selection.md`.
>
> Hard boundary: **“文学有什么用？” is a present researcher umbrella, not a historical actor formulation.** In much of the evidence below, the actor object is `poesy`, `poetry`, or `art`, not the modern institutional category `literature`.

## 0. Provisional verdict

**Methodologically excellent, but high duplication and category-instability risk.**

This candidate is unusually good for testing Problem History because a modern reader can compress several centuries into one perennial question — “what is literature for?” — while the historical texts repeatedly change:

- the object (`poesy` / `poetry` / `art` / books / literary study);
- the threatening counter-object (moral corruption, philosophy/history, science and utility, weakened religion, moralized criticism);
- the stakes (virtue, civic life, imagination, cultural authority, aesthetic autonomy);
- the answer space (educate/move, reject obsolescence, replace or supplement older authorities, refuse utility as the criterion).

A source chain from Sidney (1595) to Wilde (1891) spans **296 years** and already contains at least three plausible transformations plus a strong counter-formulation. The main weakness is not source scarcity but **prior-art saturation**: histories of literary criticism, histories of the idea of literature, Romantic critical history, and modern “uses of literature” scholarship are already mature fields.

This candidate should only become the repository pilot if Problem History adds something stricter than another history of criticism. The best possible increment would be to adjudicate **problem identity, split, displacement, and object change** across episodes that older histories often place in one critical tradition.

A narrower pilot — for example the 1820 Peacock / 1821 Shelley controversy plus one earlier and one later episode — may be stronger than a universal “use of literature” history.

## 2. Source chain and actor formulations

## Episode lead A — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy* (composed c. 1581–82; published 1595)

### Primary source

Philip Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy / A Defence of Poesie*.

Open text:

- Project Gutenberg: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1962/1962-h/1962-h.htm

Edition / context control:

- Cambridge, *The Memory Arts in Renaissance England*, “Philip Sidney, The Defence of Poesy (1595)”: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/memory-arts-in-renaissance-england/philip-sidney-the-defence-of-poesy-1595/D2EE0E67BFE485B6B16556DC28D17081

Cambridge dates composition probably to 1581–82 and treats Stephen Gosson's *The School of Abuse* (1579), dedicated to Sidney and attacking poetry on moral grounds, as part of the immediate polemical context.

### Actor-explicit formulation

Sidney defines poesy as imitation whose end is to **“teach and delight”**. More importantly for problem structure, he places poetry inside a hierarchy of learning whose final end is not knowledge alone but **virtuous action**. He compares poetry's capacity to teach and move with philosophy and history.

The actor question is therefore not safely paraphrased as the abstract modern question “what is literature useful for?” It is closer to:

> under attack, how can poesy justify its place among forms of learning by showing that it moves people toward virtue?

### Problem structure

```yaml
object:
  actor_term: poesy / poetry
  not_equivalent_by_default: modern institutional Literature
stakes:
  - moral legitimacy
  - educational rank among forms of learning
  - capacity to move people toward virtuous action
counter_objects:
  - moral/philosophical precept
  - historical example
  - anti-poetic / anti-theatrical accusations
answer_space:
  - poetry teaches and delights
  - poetic imitation moves more effectively than bare precept
  - abuses by bad practitioners do not establish that the art is bad
```

### Evidence strength

- **High** for Sidney's actor wording: accessible primary text with stable internal passage location.
- **High** for the c. 1581–82 / Gosson contextual lead: Cambridge scholarly edition/context note.
- **Not yet direct primary evidence** for Gosson's exact formulation in this packet; his 1579 text should be verified directly before building a full exchange-level episode.

### Hindsight warning

Do not replace `poesy` with a supposedly timeless category `literature` without argument. Do not translate Sidney's hierarchy of learning into a modern claim about measurable educational outcomes.

## Episode lead B — Peacock 1820 / Shelley 1821: obsolescence versus imaginative-social necessity

This is probably the strongest candidate episode because it is a **direct historical controversy** rather than two texts made adjacent by a later syllabus.

### B1. Thomas Love Peacock, “The Four Ages of Poetry” (1820)

Authoritative modern edition/context:

- Cambridge edition in *Nightmare Abbey*, Appendix C: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/nightmare-abbey/four-ages-of-poetry-1820/A8D0F758CB955A53DBD0B220CD0A1D1E

Cambridge records that the essay appeared anonymously in the first and only number of *Olliers Literary Miscellany* in summer 1820. It also records that the essay prompted Shelley to write *A Defence of Poetry* in February–March 1821.

Hilary Fraser's chapter in *The Cambridge Companion to Victorian Poetry* reproduces Peacock's deliberately provocative description of the modern poet as a **“semi-barbarian in a civilized community”** and summarizes the anti-Romantic historicist argument that poetry has become backward-looking in a civilization whose intellectual energies have moved elsewhere.

- Cambridge: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-victorian-poetry/victorian-poetry-and-historicism/B00838AE31DA2598425CC3823FA730D4

### Actor formulation — provisional pending first-edition facsimile check

Peacock's problem is not Sidney's moral legitimacy problem. The operative challenge is historical obsolescence:

> if modern civilization increasingly organizes serious intellectual work through science, philosophy, political economy, and other knowledge practices, what claim remains for the modern poet?

This is currently reconstructed from a scholarly edition plus scholarly quotation/context. Before promoting every clause to `explicit_actor`, verify the 1820 periodical text or a page-stable critical edition.

### B2. Percy Bysshe Shelley, *A Defence of Poetry* (written 1821)

Primary source:

- Project Gutenberg HTML: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5428/pg5428-images.html

### Actor-explicit counter-formulation

Shelley explicitly takes up the language of utility and argues against conceding the civic/intellectual field to “reasoners and mechanists.” His response does not simply say that poetry has one more practical use alongside chemistry or economics. It changes what counts as utility and what poetry is.

Key actor moves in the primary text:

- poetry in a broad sense is the expression of imagination;
- imagination is central to moral sympathy — the capacity to enter another's pains and pleasures;
- narrow calculating utility is inadequate to judge poetry;
- poetry is especially needed when external/material accumulation outruns internal imaginative capacity;
- poets can function as unacknowledged legislators in the broad formation of social possibility.

### Problem structure

```yaml
object:
  - poetry in an unusually extended sense
threat:
  - claim that reason / mechanism / practical knowledge has rendered poetry secondary or obsolete
stakes:
  - moral imagination
  - social sympathy
  - relationship between material/scientific progress and internal human development
presuppositions:
  - imagination is constitutive, not decorative
  - utility has more than a narrow material/calculative meaning
answer_space:
  - accept poetry's obsolescence
  - defend poetry as pleasant but nonessential
  - redefine poetry/imagination as a condition of moral and social life
```

### Evidence strength

- **High** for Shelley's actor formulation: full open primary text.
- **High** that Peacock's 1820 essay prompted Shelley's 1821 response: Cambridge critical-edition context.
- **Medium-high** for this packet's detailed Peacock formulation until the first-edition / critical-edition text is page-verified directly.

### Relation to Sidney — hypothesis only

Continuity evidence:

- both defend poetry against a challenge to its legitimacy;
- both connect poetry to ethical/social effects rather than mere ornament.

Discontinuity evidence:

- Sidney's field is learning, imitation, moral education, philosophy/history, and anti-poetic accusation;
- Peacock/Shelley organize the controversy around historical progress, science/mechanism, utility, imagination, and possible obsolescence;
- Shelley broadens the object `poetry` far beyond verse and makes imagination structurally prior to many social forms.

Current candidate relation: **`transformed_successor` or `analogy_only` remain open; do not freeze.**

## Episode lead C — Matthew Arnold, “The Study of Poetry” (1880)

### Primary text / stable historical transcription

Matthew Arnold, introduction to T. H. Ward, *The English Poets* (1880), later known as “The Study of Poetry”.

- Historical Poetics / University of Wuppertal transcription: https://lyriktheorie.uni-wuppertal.de/lyriktheorie/texte/1880_arnold2.html
- Poetry Foundation archival presentation: https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/69374/the-study-of-poetry

### Actor-explicit formulation

Arnold says poetry should be conceived as having **higher uses and destinies** than commonly assigned to it, and predicts that people will increasingly turn to poetry to **interpret**, **console**, and **sustain** life. He links this claim to limits or weakening in science, religion, and philosophy as sufficient authorities and immediately couples high cultural responsibility with the need for strict judgment of what counts as genuinely great poetry.

The actor problem is therefore not simply “is poetry morally useful?” or “is poetry obsolete?” It is closer to:

> if inherited religious/philosophical authorities no longer sustain life in the same way, what cultural authority can high poetry exercise, and how do we distinguish poetry capable of that role?

### Problem structure

```yaml
object:
  - poetry
  - specifically poetry of sufficiently high order
stakes:
  - interpretation of life
  - consolation / sustenance
  - cultural authority
  - standards of judgment / canon formation
historical_conditions_invoked_by_actor:
  - science is insufficient by itself
  - much religion and philosophy will lose authority in their current forms
answer_space:
  - assign poetry a higher future cultural role
  - enforce a high standard / distinguish classics from inferior work
```

### Evidence strength

**High** for actor wording. The 1880 transcription preserves page markers into the original introduction and provides a strong locator for the opening claim.

### Relation to Peacock/Shelley — hypothesis only

The shared noun `poetry` is insufficient. Arnold's stakes include future cultural authority, canon/evaluation, and the weakening of religion/philosophy. Those are not simply Shelley's 1821 imagination-versus-calculation controversy repeated.

A possible transformation hypothesis is:

```text
poetry versus obsolescence under science/utility
        ↓ ?
poetry as a future cultural authority amid weakened older authorities
```

But direct historical recognition/transmission evidence needs to be tested; do not infer continuity from canonical proximity.

## Episode lead D — Oscar Wilde, Preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1891): refusing moral utility as the criterion

### Primary source

Oscar Wilde, Preface, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*.

- Project Gutenberg HTML: https://www.gutenberg.org/files/174/174-h/174-h.htm

The text distinguishes well/badly written books from moral/immoral books and ends with the aphorism **“All art is quite useless.”**

### High-quality contextual reconstruction

Caroline Sumpter, “‘No Artist Has Ethical Sympathies’: Oscar Wilde, Aesthetics, and Moral Evolution,” *Victorian Literature and Culture* 44.3 (2016), DOI 10.1017/S1060150316000127:

- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/victorian-literature-and-culture/article/no-artist-has-ethical-sympathies-oscar-wilde-aesthetics-and-moral-evolution/D6B973FED02469E86EC7988F9131309E

Cambridge scholarship on the novel's reception also records the moral criticism surrounding the 1890 magazine publication and Wilde's defence of artistic freedom before the 1891 book version.

### Actor formulation

This is best used as a **counter-formulation**, not as evidence that Wilde believed art has no effects, meanings, social consequences, or value.

The historical move is to reject a particular demand placed on art: that books should be classified and justified primarily through moral approval/disapproval or direct utility.

A cautious actor-level paraphrase:

> must an artwork justify itself by moral usefulness, or can artistic value be judged on aesthetic/formal grounds not reducible to moral instruction?

### Problem structure

```yaml
object:
  - art / books, broader than poetry
stakes:
  - autonomy of artistic judgment
  - relation between art, morality, criticism, and audience
counter_frame:
  - moral/immoral classification as the primary criterion of a book
answer_space:
  - defend moral usefulness
  - deny art's legitimacy
  - reject moral utility as the governing criterion of artistic value
```

### Evidence strength

- **High** for the Preface's actor wording.
- **High** for the existence of a moral/aesthetic reception controversy around *Dorian Gray* from Cambridge scholarship.
- **Medium** for any broader claim that Wilde represents a unified “aesthetic movement” position; that requires a wider actor corpus and should not be inferred from one Preface.

### Relation to Arnold — intentionally unresolved

This is a useful adversarial pair:

```text
Arnold: very high cultural function + strict standards
Wilde: refusal of moral usefulness as the criterion of art
```

It would be a mistake to encode this as a simple linear progression from “useful” to “useless”. Possible relations include `split`, `displaced`, `analogy_only`, or multiple contemporaneous problem fields. Wilde's aphorism is especially dangerous for LLMs because it invites literal extraction without reconstructing what notion of `use` is being rejected.

## 4. Why this candidate is good for Problem History

### 4.1 The object itself changes

A modern umbrella silently treats `poesy`, `poetry`, `art`, `books`, and `literature` as one object. Historical work must instead ask whether actors themselves recognized those substitutions and what was gained/lost by them.

### 4.2 Critics disagree about the criterion, not only the answer

The Peacock/Shelley exchange can turn on what counts as `utility`; Arnold changes the expected cultural role; Wilde attacks moral utility as a governing criterion. This is exactly the kind of material in which a Problem Episode can change because the **test for an acceptable answer changes**.

### 4.3 The candidate contains real counter-formulations

Rather than a lineage of defenders saying “literature is useful”, the chain contains:

- moral defence;
- obsolescence critique;
- redefinition of utility;
- high-cultural substitution claim;
- aesthetic-autonomy refusal.

This makes `split`, `transformed_successor`, `displaced`, and `analogy_only` genuinely usable relations.

## 5. Hindsight risks

1. **Modern-Literature backprojection** — treating every premodern `poesy` / poetry debate as debate about today's institutional category `Literature`.
2. **Perennial-use question** — rewriting every defence/attack as the same “what is literature for?” question.
3. **Canonical syllabus = historical lineage** — Sidney, Shelley, Arnold, Wilde appear in histories of criticism; that does not prove actor-side transmission or problem identity.
4. **Utility flattening** — treating `use`, `utility`, moral formation, cultural authority, pleasure, and aesthetic value as equivalent variables.
5. **Wilde literalism** — reading “All art is quite useless” as “art has no effects or value”.
6. **Romantic teleology** — treating Shelley as the inevitable transition from mimetic to expressive theory because a later critical history organizes him that way.
7. **Arnold secularization shortcut** — turning his remarks on science/religion/philosophy into a generic modern “religion declined, literature replaced it” thesis without actor- and institution-specific evidence.
8. **Winner's-canon bias** — treating later canonical survival as evidence that one formulation historically defeated another.
9. **Poetry = novel = art** — using lexical family resemblance to bridge object changes without an identity argument.
10. **Effects = purpose** — inferring an actor's formulation from effects later readers or researchers attribute to literature.

## 8. Emergence / disappearance leads

This survey does **not** yet support `became_unaskable` for any episode.

Two promising research questions are better than searching for silence:

### A. When did “literature” become an actor-available object for this kind of question?

Do not assume that because a modern history can compare Sidney and Wilde, they share one object. Widdowson/Marino and the wider historiography of the category should be used as source maps, then tested against historical vocabulary and institutions.

### B. Does one justification disappear, or does its responsibility move?

For example, Sidney's moral-educative defence may cease to organize later debates in the same form without poetry/literature ceasing to have moral claims made for it. The correct transition may be `split` or `displaced`, not “solved”.

No disappearance claim should be made until positive transition evidence exists in criticism, education, publishing, institutions, or audience controversy.
